Game designers have a word that most web developers have never heard: conveyance. It's how a game tells you what to do without telling you. No popup, no arrow, no voice in your ear. The level is built so you figure it out yourself.

I make games and I make websites, sometimes on the same afternoon, and conveyance is where the two jobs ask the same question and give opposite answers.

How a game teaches you

The best explanation of conveyance I've ever seen is this video breaking down Mega Man X. If you watch one thing about game design, make it that one. Here's the short version of why Mega Man X is the example everyone reaches for.

The game drops you onto a wrecked highway with no tutorial. Enemies come from the right, so you go right and shoot them. The first time you jump against a wall, X doesn't drop straight down. He slides, slowly, which tells you the wall is something you can use, so you press jump again and you're kicking up walls before anyone told you it was a move. At the end of the stage, Vile shows up in a giant mech and beats you, and there's nothing you can do about it. Then Zero arrives and blows the mech's arm off with one shot. Nobody says "you're weak right now, and this is what strong looks like." The game shows you, then gives you the rest of the game to get there. There isn't one word of instruction in any of it.

Newer games use the same trick with more tools:

  • Paint. Yellow paint on a ledge means you can climb it. So many games do this now that players argue about it online, which tells you it works a little too well.
  • Light. Put a lamp over the door you want people to walk through. Players head for light like moths.
  • Shape. Round, soft shapes read as safe. Spikes, sharp angles and jagged outlines read as danger before you know what the thing does. A good enemy design tells you it hurts from across the room.
  • Animation. An enemy that winds up for half a second before it swings is teaching you when to dodge. A door that rattles as you walk past is telling you it opens later.

And games want you to experiment. They leave room to mess up because messing up is cheap: you die, you respawn ten seconds back, you try the other thing. Working it out is the fun part. If the game hands you the answer, it takes the fun with it, which is why people complain about the yellow paint. It's conveyance turned up so loud it solves the puzzle for you.

I'm chewing on this with Contract Zero right now, where the only way to win is to throw stuff into things. The game has to get you to look at a crate and think "I could throw that" without a popup that says PRESS E TO THROW CRATE. If I say it out loud, I take away the moment where you'd have figured it out.

How a website teaches you

Now flip it. Nobody opens a checkout page hoping for a challenge.

The person on a website came to do something: buy the thing, book the appointment, find the phone number, reset their password. Every second they spend working out the interface is a second they aren't doing what they came for. UX people call that cognitive load, and most of the job is getting it as close to zero as you can.

The tools look a lot like the game ones, but they point the other way:

  • Visual hierarchy instead of level design. The most important thing on the page is the biggest, brightest, highest-contrast thing on it. You don't hide the Buy button behind a puzzle.
  • Convention instead of discovery. The logo in the top left goes home. Underlined blue text is a link. A magnifying glass is search. Games invent new rules all the time; websites do best when they use the rules everyone already knows. A clever new kind of dropdown is one more thing your visitor has to learn.
  • Catching errors instead of respawning. If you type your email wrong, a good form tells you right there, next to the box, in plain words, before you hit submit, and it keeps everything else you typed. A bad one wipes the form and puts "Invalid input" in a red bar at the top.

A game expects you to fail a few times, and the failing is part of what you paid for. A website that lets you fail has a bug. In a game, dying teaches you something. On a website, an error teaches people to close the tab.

Where they meet

Underneath, it's one skill. Both are about teaching someone who will never read the manual. Both run on the same signals: what stands out, what looks like you can press it, what a shape or a color means before anybody explains it. A big red button means "important, maybe dangerous" in a boss fight and on a delete-account screen.

They also steal from each other constantly. A game's menus, inventory and settings screen are plain UX work, and a settings menu that fights you can sour a good game before you've played it. Going the other way, the best websites borrow from game tutorials: show one thing at a time, let people try it with nothing at stake, and react the instant something happens. A button that visibly presses in when you click it is the same idea as a sword swing that makes a sound. That's the good kind of gamification. The bad kind is a badge for logging in.

When I can't tell which mode I'm in, I ask who's supposed to do the work. In a game, the player does, and my job is to make the work fair and make sure they can see what they've got to work with. On a website, I do the work so the visitor doesn't have to.

If you play games

Every time you've said a game "explained things well" when it never explained anything, that was conveyance. It's the same craft that puts the search bar where your eyes go first. You already know what it looks like done badly. You've fought a cookie banner.

If you build websites

Before you design your next form, play the first ten minutes of a game you've never touched and notice every time it teaches you something, and how. Then take the clear signals and the instant feedback back to the form with you, and leave the dying in the game.
