History never interested me as a kid. It was dates and names and "the causes of the war were economic, political and social," in that order, on the test. Nobody in those textbooks seemed like a person. They were statues that signed treaties.

As an adult I figured out the thing school never said: nobody knows what they're doing. Nobody ever has. The people who built the foundations of this country were exactly the same, a room full of people winging it, arguing, holding grudges and hoping it would work out. Once you see history that way, it stops being homework and starts being the funniest group chat ever written.

The video that started it

The spark was IF WW2 WAS A DISCORD CHAT (HISTORICALLY ACCURATE) by Server History. Go watch it. The whole war is countries typing at each other in a Discord server, and it's somehow more accurate than half of what I remember from school. Poland asking Germany why there are tanks in its country is a better summary of September 1939 than any chapter I was assigned.

I take zero credit for that script. The World War II chat in War Chats is theirs, joke for joke, and it says so in the chat itself.

What I added

I loved that video so much I wanted to watch it with the map open. Every time a country said something, I wanted to see where it was, who it was talking to, and what the borders looked like that week. So that's what War Chats is: the chat on one side and a war-room map on the other.

  • The map follows the conversation. It flies to wherever the chat is happening and tags whoever is talking.
  • The borders change as the war does. Countries change color when they get invaded, switch sides or stop existing.
  • Every scene has notes. A little card with what really happened: the date, the numbers, the part the joke is about. You get the joke, then you get the context, and the context is usually even funnier.
  • The clock is real. Each war runs from midnight to midnight in chat time, so the time stamps line up with the calendar. A country going quiet for three hours means something.

Then I kept going

Once the WWII one worked I wanted more, so I started writing my own. The Roman Empire and the Korean War are my scripts, written in the same style, and they're both in now. The other servers you'll see (World War I, the Pacific, the Three Kingdoms, the Boer War, the Cold War, the War on Terror) are set up and waiting for their scripts.

Writing them is how I found out how much material there is. You don't have to make history funny. You only have to stop making it sound dignified.

  • Rome threw out its last king in 509 BC and swore it would never have another one. About five hundred years later Augustus ran the whole thing alone and was very careful never to call himself a king. Everyone just agreed not to bring it up.
  • In 193 AD the Praetorian Guard, the people whose whole job was protecting the emperor, killed one and then sold the empire to the highest bidder. The winner lasted 66 days.
  • The line that split Korea in 1945 was picked by two American officers with a National Geographic map, in about half an hour, late at night. It was supposed to be temporary. It's still there.
  • In 1943 Mussolini's own council voted against him and the next afternoon his own king had him arrested on the palace steps.

None of that is a joke I made up. That's just what happened.

Countries have personalities

This is the lens that made it all click for me. A country is as smart, petty, brave or stubborn as the people steering it at the time, and it shows. Countries hold grudges. They make the same mistake twice because the people who remembered the first time retired. They make promises they know they won't keep. They show up late and act like they were there all along. Switzerland spends two thousand years in the corner saying nothing. You've met every one of these people. You've been in a group chat with them.

It works both ways, too. If the people who built nations were improvising, then you're allowed to be improvising. Nobody had a manual. They had a meeting, a bad idea and a deadline, same as the rest of us.

Give it a try
